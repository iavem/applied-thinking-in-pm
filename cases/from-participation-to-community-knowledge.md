# From Participation to Community Knowledge

### Reflection, Dialogue, and Research in Environmental Program Design

**Applied Thinking | Case Study 01**

**Program Redesign · Critical Adult Learning · Research · Evaluation**

*Community-Based Environmental Education Program | Northern California | 2026*

---

## The Question

**How can environmental education help participants recognize, develop, and communicate their knowledge?**

Community programs often rely on workshops, guided activities, and surveys to support learning. These methods can provide valuable information, but they do not always create opportunities for participants to examine their experiences together or consider how their knowledge might inform future work.

This case examines how I redesigned a community-based environmental education program to connect field learning with reflection, dialogue, and evaluation.

The project also informed my thinking about participatory program design: how organizations can learn from participants without treating them solely as recipients of services or sources of feedback.

---

## 01 / Context

The program introduced adults to local wetlands through environmental education, guided observation, and group activities.

Participants explored ecological concepts, environmental change, biodiversity, and the relationship between natural environments and nearby communities.

My role involved supporting program delivery and improving its educational framework.

I identified an opportunity to strengthen the connection between participants' experiences and the way those experiences were discussed, interpreted, and evaluated.

Rather than approach each activity as a separate learning event, I developed a sequence that connected field observation with reflection and communication.

---

I led the redesign and implementation of the program's learning framework.

My work included:

- Developing curricula and educational activities.
- Organizing learning sequences and supporting program delivery.
- Designing reflection exercises and discussion formats.
- Developing participant feedback and evaluation tools.
- Reviewing feedback to inform program improvements.
- Researching approaches to participatory environmental learning.

This case focuses on my contributions to program design and evaluation. It does not reproduce organizational curricula, internal reports, participant records, or unpublished research data.

## 03 / Program Redesign
### Connecting Experience with Critical Reflection

The program offered opportunities to learn about local environments through observation and educational activities. I saw a need to strengthen what happened after those experiences: how participants interpreted what they learned and connected it to their own knowledge.

I redesigned the learning approach using principles from critical adult education and participatory research. Rather than treat learning as the transfer of information, I developed activities that encouraged participants to examine ideas, compare perspectives, and communicate their understanding.

Two considerations guided my decisions. First, accessibility should support rigorous thinking, not replace it with simplified content. Second, participant feedback should do more than measure satisfaction; it can reveal how people understand a program and where its design needs attention.

The work required adapting theoretical ideas to the program's educational goals, available resources, and conditions of delivery.

**The central insight:** A program's value depends not only on the information it provides, but also on the opportunities it creates for people to interpret and use that information.

This account describes the reasoning behind the redesign. Original curriculum materials, evaluation instruments, and implementation procedures are not included.

## 04 / Research and Theoretical Foundations

The redesign drew on several interdisciplinary perspectives.

These frameworks helped me think about learning as more than the transfer of information.

### Critical adult learning

Adult learners bring experiences, existing knowledge, and questions into an educational setting.

I used structured reflection and discussion to connect new material with those experiences.

This approach allowed participants to examine ideas, compare perspectives, and develop their understanding through dialogue.

### Black feminist thought and situated knowledge

Black feminist thought examines how lived experience, social context, and power shape the recognition of knowledge.

This perspective informed a question central to the redesign:

**Whose knowledge is recognized during a program, and how do participants have opportunities to develop and communicate it?**

I treated reflection and group discussion as substantive parts of learning rather than secondary activities.

The aim was to create opportunities for participants to interpret their experiences and engage with others' perspectives.

### Intersectionality

Intersectionality provides a framework for examining how social and institutional conditions interact.

In program design, participation may be shaped by language, work schedules, transportation, accessibility, and familiarity with institutional settings.

These considerations informed my attention to clear communication, flexible learning activities, and the practical conditions of participation.

The purpose was not to reduce intellectual complexity, but to make rigorous discussion more accessible.

### Feminist praxis

Feminist praxis connects reflection with action.

I used this principle to design activities that moved beyond recalling information.

Participants were encouraged to examine their experiences, discuss their interpretations, and practice communicating their knowledge.

The broader aim was to make learning useful beyond the immediate educational activity.

## 05 / Evaluation and Learning

I developed participant feedback tools and reflection activities to examine how people experienced the redesigned learning process.

This work helped connect program delivery with a more systematic approach to evaluation.

The evaluation considered questions such as:

- Which activities helped participants engage with unfamiliar concepts?
- How did structured discussion support reflection?
- What did participant feedback suggest about the learning experience?
- Which parts of the framework warranted further development?

The process informed my understanding of how reflection, dialogue, and peer learning could complement environmental education.

**Evidence limitation:** This public case study does not include unpublished survey findings or participant-level information. It therefore does not claim a measured change in knowledge, confidence, or behavior.

A stronger outcome evaluation would require consistent measures, an appropriate comparison over time, and evidence sufficient to support specific conclusions.

---

## 06 / From Learning to Organizational Questions

The project raised questions about how organizations use information gathered from the people participating in their programs.

Collecting feedback is one step.

Interpreting it, communicating what was learned, and using it to guide decisions require additional work.

The experience led me to consider a broader process:

**Participant Experience → Structured Reflection → Shared Interpretation → Program Decisions → Evaluation**

The sequence is a model for thinking about feedback and organizational learning, not a claim that every stage was formally implemented in this project.

It suggests that program evaluation can be more useful when organizations explain how findings relate to future decisions.

---

## 07 / Implications for Program Management

Although this project involved environmental education, several lessons apply to other organizational settings.

### Program development

A clear learning sequence can help connect individual activities with broader program objectives.

### Organizational learning

Feedback becomes more useful when staff examine its meaning rather than treating collection as the final step.

### Process improvement

Understanding the experiences of people closest to a process can help organizations identify problems before selecting solutions.

### Change management

Discussion and peer explanation can help reveal whether a new approach is understandable and practical.

These are potential applications of the project's methods. They were not separately tested in corporate or other organizational settings as part of this case.

---

## 08 / Reflections and Limitations

The redesign strengthened the structure of the program's learning activities by connecting field experiences with reflection, discussion, and evaluation.

It also changed how I approached program design.

I became more attentive to the distinction between gathering information and creating conditions in which people can interpret and use it.

Several questions remain relevant to future work:

- How should an organization decide which forms of participant feedback influence program changes?
- What evaluation methods can distinguish short-term learning experiences from lasting outcomes?
- How can staff make meaningful changes within limited time and resources?
- How can participatory methods support decisions without promising authority that participants do not hold?

These questions point toward further research and evaluation rather than established outcomes.

---

## Conclusion

This project brought together environmental education, learning design, research, and program evaluation.

I redesigned an educational framework to connect experience with reflection, dialogue, and communication. I also developed feedback activities to inform the program's ongoing improvement.

The work reinforced a principle that continues to shape my approach:

**Effective program design requires attention not only to what people learn, but also to how they interpret, communicate, and use that knowledge.**

That principle applies to educational programs and to the broader challenge of improving how organizations work.
