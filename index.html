Case Study 01 / 2026

Rethinking how people learn and participate.

At the Multicultural Center of Marin, I redesigned and implemented the learning framework for a community-based wetlands education program supported by approximately $100K in grant funding.

Program Redesign Curriculum Development Evaluation Research
30 Participants completing the late-August assessment
90% Reported greater comfort or a shift in self-perception
87% Reported greater confidence sharing their ideas

The challenge

The wetlands program used site visits and environmental education to introduce participants to local ecology and climate resilience. I wanted to help participants connect what they were learning to their own experiences and future decisions about environmental monitoring.

That required more than collecting feedback. The learning process needed space for people to interpret the material together and communicate what mattered to them.

My approach

I redesigned the program's learning framework, introducing structured reflection, Socratic seminar-style discussions, role-playing, pair-sharing, and peer teaching.

I developed curriculum and evaluation materials, organized the sessions, coordinated delivery, and adjusted activities in response to participant feedback.

What the evidence showed

An early-August environmental-learning survey gathered responses from nine participants. At the end of August, 30 participants completed a separate follow-up assessment, along with reflection worksheets.

Following the redesign, 27 of 30 participants reported greater comfort or a change in how they saw themselves. Another 26 of 30 reported greater confidence sharing their thoughts and opinions with others.

Participants also elected two community representatives and requested involvement in discussions with environmental monitoring stakeholders.

Connecting learning to strategy

Alongside the implemented learning redesign, I developed a proposed community-engaged wetlands monitoring framework.

I brought environmental research, participant feedback, and community priorities into recommendations presented to regional partners. The recommendations encouraged a more flexible monitoring approach, which stakeholders were open to considering.

What I took from this project

A program can collect valuable information without giving people a meaningful role in what happens next. The redesign explored how learning, feedback, and shared discussion could better inform future decisions.

Evidence note: The percentages describe self-reported outcomes after the learning redesign; they do not establish that the redesign caused those changes. Early- and late-August assessments had different response counts. The educational framework was implemented, while the environmental monitoring pilot remained proposed. Grant funding does not imply personal budget ownership.

Case Study 02 / 2024–2026

Building the program behind the idea.

I co-founded and managed QNest, a youth leadership and community-engagement program at the Marin LGBTQ+ Center. The work covered both program development and ongoing operations.

Program Management Operations Partnerships Learning Design
36+ Program sessions delivered
100+ Program registrations
14 Stakeholders consulted during development

The opportunity

Gaps in local youth resources created a need for consistent programming. Developing QNest meant understanding what participants needed, establishing a workable program model, and creating the systems to support continued delivery.

Designing the program

I consulted 14 stakeholders and reviewed resources across nine school sites to inform the program's priorities.

I helped establish its structure, curriculum, guidelines, and evaluation approach, then coordinated the resources and partnerships needed to put it into practice.

Managing implementation

As Program Manager, I coordinated scheduling, recruitment, participant engagement, program spending, and relationships with schools and community organizations.

I also developed a participant-tracking system and used attendance trends and feedback to inform ongoing programming decisions.

The result

QNest delivered more than 36 sessions and recorded over 100 registrations. Sessions typically involved approximately 12–20 participants.

The project gave me experience building a program from its initial concept and managing the operational systems required to keep it running.

What I took from this project

A program needs more than a good idea. It needs clear priorities, dependable systems, and room to adjust as the people involved tell you what works.
